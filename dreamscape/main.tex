\chapter*{Prologue}

The sound of a burning hearth was the first thing Ludwig heard.
It was humming quietly and sometimes making a loud cracking noise, as if the flames were sharp teeth biting through the logs, chewing them with vigor and satisfaction.
It made Ludwig hungry, as he sat in an old leather armchair curled under a blanket.

It was a cold winter night in Norway, the winds howling outside the house walls, and Ludwig knew that a trip to the cold kitchen would drain all the warmth and happiness out of him.
Thankfully, he did not have to make it, as he suddenly found he was already holding a tuna sandwich in his right hand.
It is a good feeling indeed, to recognize a hard problem and then watch it unravel all by itself.
Sadly, though, Ludwig's time was nearly running out, and there was an alarm at the back of his mind ringing louder and ever louder.
And he knew that he would soon wake up and leave the house and struggle against the frosty winds on his way to school.
It was unfair, terribly unfair, that circumstances should have aligned so perfectly, only for Ludwig to be drawn out of this dream and into mundane reality, where nothing was ever perfect.
Just before fading away, the remnants of Ludwig's consciousness were filled with anger and defiance, and he willed only for one thing---to keep the sandwich, the gift of this merciful world.

\sep

But it was too late.
Sharply Ludwig opened his eyes and sat up in his bed, all sleepiness drained out of him.
The memories from the dream were still clear in his mind, but now they seemed fake and impossible.
% Ludwig's house had neither a hearth, nor any leather chairs.
Feeling slightly disappointed, he reached out with his right hand to silence the alarm that kept ringing, but---oh wonder!---his hand was holding a tuna sandwich, just as beautiful as he'd remembered it.
Ludwig turned it over and saw that it was just a regular sandwich, yet somehow it felt both alien and familiar at the same time.
He could have pondered the reasons and consequences of what had just happened, but instead he did what any prudent 9-year-old would do---he quickly ate the sandwich and started packing his bag for school.\\

Not long after, a similar event occurred, and this time it shook humanity to its core.
A young forensic analyst named Paul Mason finally got home at eleven p.m. after a hellish day at work.
He had just moved to South Carolina, and he had just examined his first crime scene.

He went into this line of work because he had always been good at noticing details, and his life had always felt too ordinary, so he'd decided to add a little spice by connecting his career with crime.
However, after seeing the site of a gruesome murder deep in the national forest, after methodically examining the layer of blood splashed over the bushes, after counting the poor girl's broken teeth and noticing her torn clothing and the bruising around her crotch, he was starting to seriously doubt his career choices.

Paul could only console himself by thinking of the forest.
He had seen its full glory from the helicopter on his way to the crime scene.
An ocean of green, stretching into infinity, calm and immortal.
What was this murder, but a tiny drop of crimson-red death in a vast, boundless sea of green growing life?
As long as those mighty trees stood tall and strong, he thought, he could also find the strength to do this job.
Because he had chosen it, and because someone had to do it.

Feeling broken but resolute, Paul lay down on his bed and closed his eyes, immediately falling into sleep.

\sep

The green wall of nature closed around him once again.
Paul was back at the crime scene, but he could not see the body, nor any other signs of the terrible thing that had occurred there.
\emph{Perhaps a bear took the body and a rain washed away the blood,} he thought.
It relieved Paul to see that all of this was part of the circle of life, but also made him sad, because that girl had not deserved to die there, in fear and pain.
She had been a human being, not an animal to be preyed upon and brutalized and devoured.
\emph{Nature's way is cruel, although in the end we all must join in it,} he thought, watching with a mix of bitterness and awe as millions of trees swayed under gusts of wind, making it look like waves in a vast ocean of green.

As Paul meandered around, he noticed the wind growing stronger and the daylight growing dimmer, the clouds above getting darker.
He saw a line of ants carrying some small white objects over the roots of a tree.
He took a closer look, and then stumbled back with a curse, breath caught in his throat, because the ants were carrying broken human teeth.

Then the first drop of rain landed on his palm, and it felt like a knife stabbing into his soul, because it was the crimson-red color of blood.
Then another red droplet landed on his shoulder, then two more on his forearm, and a feeling of primal dread slowly crept into Paul.
He could hear the trees croaking and shaking, as if trying to uproot themselves and run away, all to escape this rain which was Death itself.
He could feel the rusty smell of blood as it started to pour from above.

Soon the whole sky was red, and Paul was red, and all around the green treetops and the bushes and the grass were all drinking this red poison and becoming the same shade of crimson-red.
And Paul knew that they would never change back, that the horrible red drops had seeped and soaked into the trees and stained their very DNA.
He knew that the green color of life would never be seen again.

\sep

Paul woke up at nine a.m., his bedroom sheets drenched in sweat.
They were soaked, but not in blood, and Paul let out a sigh of relief.
It was all a dream.
Then Paul thought about the previous day and the dead girl, and felt like shit again.
\emph{At least the sky ain't raining blood,} he thought with a thin smile.

Then he opened the window curtains to let in the morning sun, and his smile instantly disappeared, and his momentary relief turned to ash.
The trees in the yard had leaves in a weird color, a familiar, but also somewhat alien shade of red.\\

Six hours before, while it was three a.m. in South Carolina, the time was eight a.m. in Berlin, twelve p.m in Astana, and four p.m. in Tokyo.
All around the eastern hemisphere people looked up from their desks, stopped in the streets, left whatever they were doing to watch all the treetops, the decorative bushes, the lawns, and the house plants slowly turn crimson-red from top to bottom, as if someone were pouring viscous blood on top of them.

For a few minutes, no one could tear their eyes away from this foreign, yet painfully familiar color.
They were like prey that stares in the deadly eyes of a predator, unable to look away and paralyzed by fear. They felt like they were looking into the eyes of Death.\\

Paul Mason came forward with a public apology the next day, but out of the few people who heard it, no one believed he was the cause of this worldwide tragedy.
Paul himself died from exhaustion a week later.
He could not allow himself to fall asleep, terrified of what his nightmares might do onto the world.\\

Since then it was not long before humanity woke up to this supernatural phenomenon, and realized that Paul had probably been telling the truth.
Videos popped up on the Internet, showing people floating above their beds while asleep, or furniture changing shape and color, or objects appearing out of thin air.
Soon there were official statements recognizing what came to be known as \emph{reverse manifestation,} and governments around the world understood that this was an existential threat to humankind.
If one man can alter the DNA of trillions of plants in his sleep, what stops someone from dreaming about killing someone they hate?
What stops them from dreaming a nuclear explosion?
Of all people in the world, politicians are by far the most hated, and they know it better than anyone.
So, when reverse manifestation made itself apparent, the powers of the world felt uniquely compelled to do something about it.
It became a knife hanging above their heads.

Reverse manifestation was a new and mysterious phenomenon, but it would surely fall into the domain of science eventually, for it was an observable part of nature and not a figment of the mind.
With no lack of funding and no lack of talent, in three mere months humanity developed a scientific framework around reverse manifestation.
It was believed that an alternate universe existed, called the Dreamscape, that was different from our own, and that one could enter only in their sleep.
To postulate the Dreamscape was necessary to prevent the violation of conservation laws: how else could an item simply appear out of nowhere, manifested by someone dreaming about it?
Since neither energy nor mass can be created or destroyed, it had to have come from somewhere.
Next came the questions of how humans could access the Dreamscape, and whether humans were the only ones that could do it, and what the laws of the Dreamscape were.
Mammals had been known to show brain activity during sleep that was similar to having dreams, and numerous experiments were conducted to try and provoke reverse manifestation in non-human animals.
These experiments made one thing clear: only humans can manifest dreams.
This conclusion led the way to the idea that reverse manifestation is intimately connected with the defining feature of the human species---consciousness.

A groundbreaking paper published two months after the Rain of Red, contained the following:

\begin{quote}
  We work on the assumption that a person's consciousness is not made of matter, nor is it a mystical thing that cannot be defined.
  It is simply a \emph{pattern} that the human brain exhibits through its activity, akin to a computer program running on hardware.
  The brain is the hardware, the mind is the program.
  This program keeps running even when the person is asleep, however it loses connection with the real world, becomes decoupled from it.
  In this state the program is replicated by the Dreamscape, through a mechanism yet unknown.
  The nature of the Dreamscape is such that events and thoughts are equivalent---to imagine something is the same as to manifest it.
  In other words, the Dreamscape is a world of patterns, a world of distortions that propagate through some unknown medium.
  It is not unreasonable that the native patterns of the Dreamscape are also a form of consciousness, in which case it might be a sentient being who is interested in replicating other sentient beings' minds in itself, a program that simulates other programs.
  Which is to say, humanity is facing a potentially hostile extraterrestrial intelligence.

  Once in the Dreamscape, a person's consciousness enters a world of experience entirely created by thought.
  The thought becomes reality in the Dreamscape, and through some unknown channel it can become reality in our own world, presumably since two instances of the same program share a link, becoming entangled like quantum particles.
  We call this link the \emph{mind tunnel.}
  The sentience behind the Dreamscape may be able to utilize it to enact change in our own universe.
  Perhaps, the Dreamscape has discovered the human civilization only recently, which is why reverse manifestation had never been recorded in history.
\end{quote}

A very fortunate discovery was made: the mind tunnel was typically very narrow, so most people were incapable of manifesting any large-scale events in their sleep.
However, there were outliers---extraordinary individuals who could raise whole cities in their sleep, who could make all cats in the world bark like dogs, or teleport mount Everest to the moon.
If the mind tunnel of the average person were one meter in diameter, Paul Mason's mind tunnel would be as wide as the city of Beijing.
It was a wild stroke of luck indeed, that Paul Mason's first manifestation was so harmless, and it was a hero's act to stay awake until his very death, for there is no saying what horrors might have come alive if Paul had allowed himself to enter the Dreamscape again.\\

As soon as scientists had developed a method of measuring the width of a person's mind tunnel, people all over the world underwent mandatory testing.
Of people like Paul Mason, there was fewer than one thousand on the planet.
These people were not to be envied, as they were hauled to special facilities for monitoring, and were forced to take experimental drugs that were supposed to prevent them from having dreams.

Yet even with the most dangerous people isolated, a threat still loomed over human society.
During the first month after Mason's Rain of Red, a prison guard fell asleep at his post and accidentally opened the electronic locks in half of the prison's cells, setting free hundreds of criminals.
A couple of weeks later, someone fell asleep on an airplane and manifested a bird that flew into its turbine and caused the airplane to crash into the ocean.
Only ten passengers survived.

Physicists, neurologists, and engineers all over the world worked late hours, preferring insomnia over the treacherous domain of the Dreamscape.
After four months since the Rain of Red, they had engineered a device, about the size of a microwave oven, which could prevent people from having dreams in an area of about thirty meters.
After another month, it was the size of a smartphone and could cover two hundred meters of radius.

This device came to be known as the \emph{dream inhibitor}. Within one week, one thousand inhibitors were produced worldwide.
Another week later, it was one million.
By the end of the third week, these devices were distributed to all major populated areas in the world.
By the greatest feat of scientific engineering and social coordination, humanity had erected a shield against the Dreamscape in less than half a year.\\

And once humanity was shielded, it would launch its own attack.

\chapter*{The Dreamscape}

The room was pierced by a bright beam of sunlight through the open window, illuminating a simple wooden table.
Photons scattered from it like little tennis balls, bounced off the carpeted floor, off the ceiling and the simple chandelier, off the long bookshelves covering the walls, and landed in the hazel eyes of a girl who sat on the floor.
There was a textbook in her lap, and a small cat sleeping on the carpet next to her.
The book had no pictures, only words, but they were all meaningless, no more than random lines of ink.
They looked like vines that twisted and grew, spreading leaves and branches and more leaves from those branches.

Without a shadow of surprise, the girl saw letters sprout from the page, forming green stems and extending further and further, burrowing into the floor with twisted roots.
All around her life was stirring.
The table turned and shifted, and its dead wooden frame gave birth to dozens of living branches.
All of them bloomed with leaves and flowers, extending towards the sun, drinking the yellow sunlight.
Like a party of green fairies they were dancing in the air.
The cat awoke and studied the plants curiously, then started playing with the leaves.

Little islands of moss and lichen emerged from the carpet, like drops of green and white paint on a brown canvas, and the lower bookshelves hid behind dense raspberry bushes.
Vines of ivy covered the walls, painting marvellous patterns in the green colors of the forest. The girl walked across the room to the window, admiring the beautiful assembly of trunks, leaves, and flowers.
She looked outside, covering her eyes from the light, and saw a quick water stream running between two shores of green grass. There were children holding fishing rods, awkwardly throwing line in the stream.

She turned her gaze inside the room once again, and saw that she had cast a long and dark shadow on the leaves, taking light away from them, and it made her a little sad.

Just a moment later, this sadness grew out of all proportion, as if someone flipped a switch inside her head.
The girl looked at all those plants and thought that maybe they should just \emph{die.}
Her sadness turned to anger, hot and furious and alien, and she saw the life around her burst into flame.
The mossy floor was burning, the vines were writhing in crimson-red fire.
There was no rhythm to the dancing flames, no music, only death and malevolence.
Green, fragile stems became black as charcoal, leaves were turning to ash in the blink of an eye.
Bushes came ablaze and writhed and withered.

The cat curled in the center of the room and gave quiet meows of fear that drowned in the deafening roar of fire.
But once the flames tasted its fur, the cat jumped with a desperate screech and darted from corner to corner, slowly dying in agony as the blazing teeth of fire sank deeper into its tiny body.

Tears evaporated from the girl's eyes before they could slide down her cheek, but then the burning pain in her feet became unbearable, and the world disappeared, like a television screen cut from electricity.

\sep

Susan awoke in distress.
The house was quiet, the dawn was just about to break from behind the dense forest that surrounded the village.
She woke up so early, yet there was no trace of sleep in her mind.
Could she be nervous because of today's return trip to London, or was it the scary dream she had seen?
The dream left a weird impression.
At first, things had gone pretty normally, she reckoned.
The place had been her childhood room, in an apartment that her family had long moved away from.
She was told to study for her physics class recently, so that explained the textbook she'd been reading in her dream.
The growing vegetation must have been an influence of her past three months---Susan had spent the entire summer in the wilderness of southern England, breathing the smell of pines and fishing in water streams and climbing trees.
It was a life of adventure she had led.
She pretended to be the last person on an Earth overtaken by aliens, left to survive in the woods on her own.
She pretended she could control the gusts of wind and talk to animals, and that she had a great destiny to fulfil.
She was a predator, swift and soundless in the brush of the forest, broken apart from civilization and living by her own laws.
However, she still had her phone with her whenever she went into the forest, because otherwise her mother would get mad.

Susan had a wild and colorful imagination, one that was beating inside her like a second heart, striving to become reality.
This heart could beat so fast that it would be hard for Susan to breathe, but her parents were always there to bring down the blood pressure, which is why half of her room was taken up by weird branches and stones she had found in the woods, while the other half contained books, pencils and notepads.
For all of Susan's imagination, her parents did not seem to share it, because the only way they knew of making Susan do something useful, was to give her a book and tell her to read.
She was not angry at them, however, because she understood that reading was important, and because they, in turn, understood the importance that she placed in fantasy.
Besides Tolstoy, Twain, Feynman, and Dawkins, they also bought her Wrede, Rowling, Tolkien and Martin.
All in all, Susan thought, she was pretty educated for a girl about to enter fifth grade.

Now, she had a couple of hours to spare before her mom and dad would wake up, so she put on some clothes and strolled out into the morning forest.
Her dream wouldn't leave her mind. 
Susan remembered seeing her school classmates fishing outside her window, which was also not surprising.
After all, it was only a week before the start of school, and she supposed she missed her friends enough to see them in a dream. 
But then...
A blazing, roaring maw of crimson fire had swallowed her and all the beautiful plants that grew about her, and Susan could not understand why.
She knew that dreams took root in one's own memories, fears, desires and emotions.
What is born of the unconscious mind, must be determined by it.
However, Susan had never seen a room on fire, it was not something she had really thought about, and surely not something she desired.
It felt like she'd been watching her dream as a movie on TV, but then someone had come and taken the remote and switched to another channel.
An alien channel, showing \emph{someone else's} dream.

It left a taste of horror in Susan's mouth.
She thought of her cat Turquoise, named so after the color of his curious thin-slitted eyes.
She remembered how he'd played with the leaves, and how he'd looked at her with wild terror when the room had turned from green to crimson-red.
She recalled how he had fought for his fading life, desperately and fruitlessly, like a little bird trapped inside a heating oven.

Susan ran through the green forest with practiced agility, jumping over roots and sliding between trunks.
Her heart was beating faster, and soon she reached one of the tallest trees in the forest, standing proudly at the top of a small hill.
A magnificent pine, with a trunk that Susan couldn't close her arms around, and thick branches spanning wide and high above her head.
With a closer look one could spot ropes and fabric and wooden panels between the high branches, hiding behind the pine needles.
A simple ladder made of rope hung from the lowest branch to the ground, spanning fifteen meters along the trunk.
Susan was a little out of breath from walking uphill, and her head felt heavy from lack of proper sleep, but she smiled and started to climb, taking every step carefully and cautiously.

Susan's tree house was mainly a large wooden platform resting on three thick horizontal branches, about twenty meters from the ground.
It was firmly secured by modern nylon rope and surrounded on every side with paracord nets, to make sure Susan couldn't fall down from the platform.
Each net was carefully set between a pair of thick living branches, using at least eight attachment points, and thick rope was passed through it for added security.
Every knot had a fallback in case it failed, and no piece of rope was hanging loose.
Above the platform, a sheet of tarpaulin canvas was placed in an upside-down V-shape, like a tent, to protect the platform from rain.
Finally, there was a simple wooden table with a chair, both having ropes attached to their legs, so they couldn't fall down from the platform.
All of that was the work of Susan's mother Iris, who had been a semi-professional mountain climber in her college years.

Susan finally reached the platform and stopped to shake the soreness from her forearms.
Susan had imagined herself the ruler of the entire forest, with this tree house her royal castle, a place high and noble, elevated above the mortal ground.
There had usually been a stack of books om the table. She would sit on her wooden throne and read stories of great honor and prowess, looking down on her kingdom from time to time.
She never brought any of her science books to her castle.
Now the table was empty, and Susan's fiction literature was packed for the trip to London.

Otherwise, everything was nearly the same as when her wooden castle had only been built.
The rope was of excellent quality, and each knot was tightened with care and skill, so the structure remained stable and secure for the past two months without adjustment.
Susan grabbed one of the surrounding branches and took another look down.
The ground was far below, and she felt her toes and fingers tingle, felt the danger and power, all the potential energy she'd gained by climbing so high.
At times like these, Susan realized she really loved her parents.
Not in the trivial way, where a child loves the parent who lets them eat as much candy as they want, but in a way that depended on the kind of people her parents were, and what she meant to them, and what they did for her.
She saw that her existence had been meant, that her parents had a plan when she was born.
It was a simple plan---to raise a well-rounded, capable, educated daughter.
Susan felt like a piece of hot steel in the hands of a skilled blacksmith who was doing his job in earnest, not just playing with his bellows and his anvil.
With every blow of the hammer Susan was taking shape, and she knew the smith felt a sort of aesthetic pride, as he saw a chunk of metal slowly transform into a beautiful blade under his hammer.
A blade whose gleaming edge reflected his own personality.
Susan thought she was made of pretty good steel, and she decided to become a strong, sharp blade, probably a western longsword or a Japanese katana.
